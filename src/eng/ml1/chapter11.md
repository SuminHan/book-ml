# Chapter 11. Reinforcement Learning Preview

In 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, one of the world's top Go
players, 4 games to 1. What's interesting is how AlphaGo learned: nobody
told it "this move is correct in this situation" — it played millions of
games against itself, gradually improving its own strategy in the
direction that increased its win count. Learning from the **outcome**
(win/lose) alone, rather than correct answers, is what fundamentally sets
this apart from the supervised learning we've covered so far.

## 11.1 Learning Without Correct Answers

Supervised learning always had a correct answer \\(y\\). But in chess,
there's no correct answer to "exactly where should move 17 be played" —
several moves could be good, and a move that looks bad right now might lead
to a better outcome later. **Reinforcement Learning (RL)** is a framework
for exactly this kind of situation: an agent interacts with an environment,
takes actions, and receives a reward as a result. The goal isn't to
maximize the immediate reward, but the **total cumulative reward**.

## 11.2 The Components of Reinforcement Learning

- **State \\(s\\)**: information describing the situation the agent is in
  (e.g., the current arrangement of a Go board, a game character's
  position).
- **Action \\(a\\)**: what the agent can choose to do in that state (e.g.,
  where to place a stone).
- **Reward \\(r\\)**: a numeric signal received immediately as a result of
  an action (e.g., +1 for winning a game, -1 for losing).
- **Policy \\(\pi\\)**: a strategy mapping states to actions —
  \\(\pi(s) = a\\) (deterministic) or \\(\pi(a|s)\\) (stochastic, the
  probability of choosing action \\(a\\) in state \\(s\\)).

The agent interacts continuously with the environment: taking action
\\(a_0\\) in state \\(s_0\\) yields reward \\(r_0\\) and a new state
\\(s_1\\), and this repeats. This sequence of state-action-reward is
called an **episode** or **trajectory**.

## 11.3 The Key Difference From Supervised Learning

| | Supervised Learning | Reinforcement Learning |
|---|---|---|
| What's given | (input, correct answer) pairs | a sequence of (state, action, reward) |
| Is there a "correct answer"? | Yes | No — only a reward |
| Where the data comes from | A fixed dataset | Generated by the agent's own actions |
| Role of time | Each sample is independent | The current action affects future states |

The single most important difference is **delayed reward**: whether move
17 in chess was good isn't known right after playing it — only once the
game ends (or much later). Figuring out how much a current action
contributed to the final outcome (the credit assignment problem) is the
core reason RL is hard.

## 11.4 Why "Cumulative" Reward

Maximizing only the immediate reward leads to shortsighted strategies — in
chess, capturing a piece right now could be a trap that leads to
checkmate a few moves later. So RL is designed to maximize **the total
reward from now until the end** (the return). Rewards far in the future
are typically discounted a little at a time with a discount factor
\\(\gamma\\) (between 0 and 1) — reflecting the common-sense idea that "a
gain right now is worth slightly more than the same gain far in the
future." This concept (discounted cumulative reward) gets formalized as
the Bellman equation in ML2 Chapter 5.

## 11.5 Preview: The Bellman Equation

Using a small 3-state MDP (Markov Decision Process — the mathematical
framework that formalizes states, actions, rewards, and transitions), we'll
compute "the value of each state" (the cumulative reward you'd expect
starting from that state) directly, by hand. The formal name for this
calculation is the **Bellman equation**, and we'll meet it again in a much
more general form in ML2 Chapter 5.

**Supervised learning learns "how to imitate the correct answer";
reinforcement learning learns "how to discover, through trial and error,
what leads to a good outcome."**
